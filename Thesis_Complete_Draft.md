# Design and Development of a Prescriptive Shot-Analytics System for Football Using Expected-Goals Concept, appropriate Machine Learning and Game-Theoretic Optimisation Techniques

---

## Abstract

Football's most-used analytical metrics, expected goals (xG) and post-shot expected goals (PSxG), describe chances and strikes after they have happened. They evaluate a shot; they do not advise the player or goalkeeper on the decision before it. This thesis designs and develops a prescriptive shot-analytics system that closes that gap. The system, called StrikeLab, contains two machine-learning models built as gradient-boosted classifiers: a pre-shot xG model with 99 spatial and contextual features trained on 500,000 professional shots, and a post-shot PSxG model with 27 features trained on one million physics-informed synthetic samples generated from goalkeeper biomechanics. On top of these sit two decision engines. A Shot Coach sweeps the goal frame and recommends the aim point of greatest expected scoring value, explicitly weighting the accuracy cost of hard, well-placed strikes. A Keeper Coach treats the striker-goalkeeper encounter as a minimax game and computes the goalkeeper position that minimises the striker's best available option, providing the constructive counterpart to the empirical finding that professional players already approximate minimax play. Both engines run at interactive speed through batched inference. The models are evaluated for both discrimination (ROC-AUC) and calibration (reliability, Brier score), and the system is examined critically: its synthetic-data basis, its simplifications, and the ethical responsibilities of a tool that recommends rather than merely measures. The thesis reports what was built, what was measured, what failed and was corrected, and what remains unverified. Its principal limitation is that the post-shot model and the prescriptive layer are validated on synthetic data rather than real match events; empirical validation on real data is identified as the decisive direction for future work.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Problem Context and Motivation

Football is the most widely followed sport in the world, played and watched across virtually every nation and sustained by a professional industry whose transfer fees, broadcasting rights and wage bills run into billions of pounds each year. Within an industry of that scale, marginal advantages carry enormous value: the difference between a chance converted and a chance squandered can decide a league title, a relegation, or a club's financial survival. Any method that sharpens decision-making around the sport's decisive moments, its chances at goal, is therefore of considerable practical and commercial importance.

Football is also a low-scoring sport, and that matters for the way it can be modelled. In professional football roughly one shot in nine or ten results in a goal (Perez-Arroniz et al., 2025), which means the event a shot model must predict is rare. A classifier trained on such data without accounting for the imbalance will tend to predict "no goal" for everything and still appear accurate, while remaining useless for the decisions that actually matter. This is not an incidental statistical nuisance; it dictates which modelling approach is defensible and which evaluation metric is honest, and it recurs throughout the design decisions reported in this thesis.

Over the past decade the sport has been reshaped by event and tracking data. The proliferation of detailed match-event datasets has given rise to a new class of metrics that quantify performance far beyond traditional counts of goals and shots. Foremost among these is expected goals (xG), which assigns to every shot the probability that an average player would score it given its context: location, angle, goalkeeper position, and the configuration of surrounding play. Its post-shot counterpart, PSxG, conditions on the ball having reached the goal frame and estimates the scoring probability from where and how hard it was struck. Together these metrics are now embedded in professional recruitment, opposition analysis and broadcast punditry (Mead, O'Hare and McMenemy, 2023). Their adoption has been remarkably fast: from a research curiosity a decade ago to a number displayed alongside goals and shots in live television coverage of most major leagues.

Yet for all their influence, these metrics share a fundamental limitation: they are descriptive. xG and PSxG measure what has already happened, namely how good a chance was, or how well a shot was struck, only after the event has taken place. They evaluate; they do not advise. A striker cannot ask an xG model where to shoot, and a goalkeeper cannot ask a PSxG model where to stand. The analytics industry has become sophisticated at measuring chances and strikes, but it has not placed decision-guiding tools in the hands of the players and coaches who face the decision itself. This gap, between the analytical sophistication now available to clubs and the prescriptive, decision-guiding tools actually placed in the hands of players and coaches, is precisely the space this thesis addresses.

The work that follows presents StrikeLab, a system that unifies pre-shot expected goals and post-shot expected goals, both implemented as gradient-boosted classifiers, with two prescriptive decision engines. The first, a Shot Coach, sweeps the goal mouth through the PSxG model and recommends the aim point that maximises the probability of scoring, explicitly accounting for the fact that harder-struck shots are less accurate, an execution-error model. The second, a Keeper Coach, treats the striker-goalkeeper encounter as a two-player game and, by solving a minimax optimisation over the striker's best responses, recommends the goalkeeper position that minimises the attacker's best available chance. The models are developed and demonstrated on a combination of a 500,000-shot professional event dataset (for xG) and a physics-informed synthetic dataset grounded in goalkeeper biomechanics and shot geometry (for PSxG); the processing pipeline is deliberately architected so that real event data can be substituted without structural change, and empirical validation on such data is identified as the principal direction for future work.

The design of the system was shaped by three properties of the problem, each of which maps onto a concrete design decision, and each of which deserves to be stated explicitly at the outset.

First, the data are complex. Goals are rare events, producing a severe class imbalance that naive classifiers handle poorly. Shot outcomes depend on continuous spatial relationships, namely the shooter's position and angle, the goalkeeper's location, and the configuration of defenders, that interact non-linearly rather than additively. The relevant signal is geometric and spatial, not a simple list of independent attributes, and any model must be designed accordingly. The practical consequence is the choice of gradient-boosted decision trees, a modelling family built precisely for tabular data with many interacting features, over more transparent linear alternatives whose expressive power is insufficient for this feature space.

Second, the problem is adversarial. Unlike predicting a passive outcome, advising a striker means advising against an intelligent opponent, the goalkeeper, who is simultaneously trying to minimise the striker's success. A recommendation of where to shoot is only as good as its assumption about where the keeper is, and a recommendation of where the keeper should stand must anticipate the striker's best response. This mutual dependence is not incidental; it is the defining structure of the problem, and it is what motivates the game-theoretic treatment at the heart of this work. A recommendation computed without reference to the opponent's likely response would be incoherent, which is why the Shot Coach and the Keeper Coach are designed as a pair rather than as independent tools.

Third, football contains irreducible uncertainty. The sport is famously low-scoring and stochastic: identical chances routinely produce different outcomes, and no model, however sophisticated, can predict a single shot's result with certainty. This is not a defect to be engineered away but a property of the domain to be represented honestly. A well-calibrated probability, accompanied by an explicit acknowledgement of the ceiling it implies, is a more useful and more truthful output than a spuriously confident prediction, and pre-empts the misplaced expectation that such a model should approach perfect accuracy. The system therefore reports probabilities with their uncertainty and their limits rather than as deterministic verdicts.

A problem with these characteristics cannot be solved by a single predictive model. It requires combining calibrated machine learning with an explicit model of the strategic interaction between attacker and goalkeeper, and communicating the results in a way that respects the domain's uncertainty. It is a problem that sits at the intersection of machine learning, decision theory and game theory, both practically motivated and academically rich, and the next sections set out the theoretical grounding for that claim before the system itself is described.

### 1.2 Decision-Making Under Uncertainty: Theoretical Foundations

The decision a footballer faces at the moment of a shot, and the decision a goalkeeper faces in anticipating it, are, at root, decisions taken under uncertainty and strategic opposition. Three established theoretical frameworks explain why these decisions are hard and why a computational approach is an appropriate response.

Expected utility theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944) provides the normative foundation. It holds that a rational agent facing uncertain outcomes should choose the action that maximises expected utility: here, the expected probability of scoring, or of preventing a goal. The Shot Coach is a direct operationalisation of this principle: it evaluates every available aim point and selects the one of greatest expected value, given the keeper's position and the shooter's execution error. It is worth being precise about what expected utility contributes here beyond common sense. The principle supplies the correct way to aggregate uncertainty: a shot aimed at the top corner may have a high probability of scoring if it goes in, but the probability that it goes in at all is low; expected utility tells us these two quantities must be multiplied, not weighed by intuition. This is the formal content of the Shot Coach's expected-value calculation, and it is why the tool is built on a decision-theoretic foundation rather than on a heuristic.

Because the striker and goalkeeper are opponents with directly opposed objectives, however, expected utility alone is insufficient, since the encounter is a game. Game theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944; Nash, 1950) analyses precisely such strategic interactions, and its minimax principle prescribes that, in a competitive setting, each player should choose the strategy that minimises the maximum advantage available to the opponent. The reasoning is subtle and worth stating plainly. In a zero-sum encounter, the striker's best aim point depends on where the keeper stands, and the keeper's best position depends on where the striker aims. Neither can be chosen in isolation. The minimax solution identifies the pair of strategies under which neither player can improve by unilaterally changing, and it is the correct normative target for a positioning recommendation precisely because the opponent is assumed to be rational and adaptive.

This is not merely a theoretical analogy for football. Palacios-Huerta (2003), in a widely cited study of penalty kicks, showed that professional players' shooting and goalkeeping choices closely approximate the mixed-strategy minimax equilibrium predicted by game theory, one of the clearest empirical confirmations of minimax play in any real-world setting. The result is striking in part because it concerns behaviour rather than prescription: players under intense pressure, with no time for calculation, behave as though they had solved the game. Comparable evidence exists in tennis, where Walker and Wooders (2001) showed that professional serve directions follow minimax predictions, and in the penalty-kick analysis of Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002), which reached a compatible conclusion while accounting for player heterogeneity. The Keeper Coach makes this connection explicit and constructive: rather than describing an equilibrium after the fact, it computes the goalkeeper position that minimises the striker's best response.

Finally, bounded rationality (Simon, 1955) explains why such optimisation is valuable in practice. Human agents cannot, in the fraction of a second available at the point of a shot and under intense physical and psychological pressure, enumerate every option and compute the optimum; they satisfice, relying on heuristics and experience that are systematically imperfect (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979). This is not a criticism of players; it is a description of the constraints under which the decision is genuinely made. A tool that computes the normative optimum offline does not replace the player's judgement, but it provides a benchmark against which real decisions can be measured and improved, turning an intractable in-the-moment calculation into an analysable one. The distinction between prescribing and evaluating is central here: the system's output is a reference point for training and reflection, not a command issued in real time.

Together, these frameworks form the intellectual foundation of the thesis: the problem is one of expected-value maximisation (decision theory) within an adversarial interaction (game theory), undertaken by agents who cannot themselves compute the optimum in real time (bounded rationality). This grounding is what distinguishes StrikeLab from a purely technical exercise. The machine-learning models are the means, but the reason the problem exists, and the reason this approach fits, are theoretical. It also explains why the ethical analysis in Chapter 3 is not an addendum: a tool built on these foundations makes claims about what players and goalkeepers should do, and a claim of that kind carries responsibilities that a descriptive metric does not.

### 1.3 From Measurement to Prescription: Data and Machine Learning in Football

Data-driven decision support under uncertainty is not unique to football. Across finance, insurance, healthcare and logistics, machine learning has been used to convert large, noisy datasets into actionable predictions, from credit-risk scoring to clinical decision support to demand forecasting (Rane et al., 2024). The common thread is the transformation of raw historical data into probabilistic guidance where fixed, deterministic rules fail. Football's analytics movement is a domain-specific instance of the same shift, and it inherits both the promise and the methodological obligations of that broader field.

Within football, expected goals is that movement's flagship. By modelling the probability that a shot becomes a goal from its spatial and contextual features, xG reframed the evaluation of chances from raw counts to calibrated probabilities. The move from "how many shots" to "how good were the shots" made it possible to compare teams and players on the quality of the chances they created and conceded, independent of the noise of individual goals. Related metrics have followed: expected assists extend the same logic to the pass preceding a shot, and post-shot expected goals assess both finishing and goalkeeping (Green, 2012; Rathke, 2017; StatsBomb, n.d.). A further generation of value models extends the idea beyond the shot event entirely, assigning value to every action on the pitch, whether or not it leads directly to a shot, in the possession-value framework of Fernandez, Bornn and Cervone (2018) and the VAEP model of Decroos, Bransen, Van Haaren and Davis (2019). Methodologically, these are supervised classification problems over predominantly tabular, spatial features with strong non-linear interactions and pronounced class imbalance, a profile for which gradient-boosted decision trees, and XGBoost in particular, are well suited, and which this thesis adopts for both its xG and PSxG models.

It is worth situating the modelling choice in the wider machine-learning literature. Gradient boosting (Freund and Schapire, 1997; Friedman, 2001) builds an ensemble of shallow decision trees sequentially, each tree learning to correct the residual errors of its predecessors. XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) is a regularised, highly engineered implementation of this idea that has become the default tool for tabular learning problems, and it has been adopted widely in football analytics precisely because shot prediction problems are tabular, feature-dense and imbalanced. The price of this performance is interpretability: an ensemble of hundreds of trees cannot be read the way a single tree or a logistic regression can. This thesis treats that trade-off explicitly rather than assuming it away, compensating at the presentation layer through feature-importance reporting and visualisations, a decision examined in the Technical Development and Findings chapters.

Crucially, however, the football-analytics literature and the tools it has produced remain overwhelmingly descriptive and evaluative. They answer "how good was this chance?" and "how well was this shot struck?", retrospective questions of measurement. They do not answer the prospective, prescriptive question a player or coach actually faces: "what should I do?" This is not an oversight in the literature so much as a difference of kind: describing a chance and prescribing a decision are different tasks, and the second requires the first to be composed with an optimisation over the decision space. This thesis sets out to bridge that gap. By building calibrated xG and PSxG models and then composing them with decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimisation, StrikeLab moves football analytics from measurement to prescription, from evaluating decisions after the event to recommending them before it. The remainder of this thesis presents the design, development and critical evaluation of that system.

### 1.4 Scope and Structure of the Thesis

The scope of the work is open-play and set-piece shooting situations in association football, the decisive moments at which chances are created and either converted or denied. The system concerns itself with the shot and its immediate preconditions: where the shot is taken, where the goalkeeper stands, how the defenders are arranged, and how the strike is executed. It does not address wider team-level questions such as shot suppression over a season or squad-level talent evaluation, though the underlying models could inform them.

The thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 sets out the research design: the aim, objectives, research questions and hypotheses, and the grounds on which the study is justified. Chapter 3 describes the agile, design-and-development methodology and the ethical framework that runs through the work. Chapter 4 reviews the technical, theoretical and practical literature, and states the gap the thesis addresses. Chapter 5 reports the technical development of the system in detail: the tools, the models, the prescriptive layer, and the challenges encountered. Chapter 6 presents the findings against the two research questions, states the conclusions, and identifies future work.

### 1.5 Terminology and Key Concepts

A short glossary of the terms used throughout this thesis is provided here so that the reader can move through the later chapters without ambiguity. The definitions are the ones the thesis itself uses; where a term has a contested or overloaded meaning in the wider literature, the specific sense intended is stated.

Expected goals (xG): the probability, estimated by a model, that a shot results in a goal, given its context. The context includes the shot's location and angle, the goalkeeper's position, and the configuration of surrounding play. An xG of 0.30 means that thirty per cent of comparable shots score. xG is a property of the chance before the ball is struck.

Post-shot expected goals (PSxG): the probability, estimated by a model, that a shot that reaches the goal frame scores, given the placement and quality of the strike. PSxG conditions on the ball being on target, so it isolates the shot itself from the chance that preceded it. PSxG is a property of the strike.

Discrimination: a model's ability to separate goals from non-goals, measured by ROC-AUC, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. AUC summarises the trade-off between true-positive and false-positive rates across all thresholds; it is threshold-independent and unaffected by class imbalance.

Calibration: the degree to which a model's predicted probabilities are truthful, measured by the Brier score and reliability diagrams. A model is calibrated if, across all its predictions of value p, the actual outcome rate is approximately p. Discrimination and calibration are independent: a model can rank shots perfectly while being systematically over- or under-confident.

Class imbalance: the situation in which one outcome class is far rarer than another. In the real shot data, goals are 9.52% of shots, so a trivial "always predict no goal" model is 90.5% accurate yet useless. Imbalance is the reason evaluation cannot rest on accuracy.

Gradient boosting: an ensemble method that builds many shallow decision trees sequentially, each correcting the residual errors of the previous ensemble. XGBoost is a regularised, engineered implementation widely used for tabular data. The models in this thesis are XGBoost classifiers.

Expected value: in decision theory, the probability-weighted average of the utility of each outcome of an action. For a shot, the expected value of aiming at a point is the probability of scoring given the aim, which the Shot Coach computes by weighting each possible realised placement by the probability of achieving it.

Minimax: in game theory, the principle that a player should choose the strategy that maximises their payoff against the opponent's best response. In a zero-sum encounter such as the shot, the goalkeeper should choose the position that minimises the striker's best available aim point.

Execution error: the discrepancy between where a striker aims and where the ball actually goes. The execution-error model represents this as a distribution centred on the aim point, with spread that grows with the difficulty of the placement.

Danger map: the system's visualisation of the PSxG model over the goal frame, colouring each point of the goal by the scoring probability of a shot placed there, given the scenario. It renders the model's reasoning visible.

Synthetic data: data generated by a rule rather than observed from the world. The post-shot model is trained on synthetic shots generated from shot geometry and goalkeeper biomechanics. Synthetic data is the thesis's principal methodological caveat.

## 2. Research Design

### 2.1 Research Aim

The primary aim of this thesis is to design, develop, and critically evaluate StrikeLab, a prescriptive shot-analytics system that unifies pre-shot expected goals (xG) and post-shot expected goals (PSxG) models with decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimisation, in order to recommend optimal shot placement and goalkeeper positioning in association football, and to critically evaluate the validity, transparency, and responsible use of such a system. This involves:

- generating and curating a spatially and physically grounded dataset of shooting situations, capturing shot geometry, goalkeeper position, defender configuration, and shot execution;
- developing calibrated xG and PSxG models as gradient-boosted classifiers, and evaluating them on both discrimination (how well they separate goals from non-goals) and calibration (whether their probabilities are truthful);
- designing a Shot Coach that recommends the aim point of greatest expected value, modelling the trade-off between beating the goalkeeper and the increased inaccuracy of harder, more precisely placed shots;
- designing a Keeper Coach that formulates goalkeeper positioning as a minimax optimisation, recommending the position that minimises the striker's best available response;
- building an interactive system that computes and communicates these outputs in real time, with an emphasis on interpretability and the honest presentation of uncertainty;
- critically evaluating the models and the prescriptive recommendations, and reflecting on the system's limitations, ethical dimensions, and responsible use.

### 2.2 Research Objectives

The objectives below map to the module's three-type taxonomy (Learn & Understand; Design & Develop; Feedback & Submit) and are each stated so that evidence of their achievement can be located within the thesis.

1. To review and understand the foundations of prescriptive shot analytics, namely expected-goals and post-shot expected-goals modelling, gradient-boosted classification, and the decision-theoretic and game-theoretic principles (expected utility, minimax, and bounded rationality) that motivate the approach.
2. To examine and evaluate existing football-analytics metrics and tools in order to identify the specific gap between descriptive shot-evaluation metrics and prescriptive, decision-guiding systems.
3. To design and develop calibrated xG and PSxG models using gradient-boosted decision trees on spatial and contextual shot features, and to evaluate their discrimination and calibration.
4. To design and develop the prescriptive layer, comprising the Shot Coach (expected-value optimisation with an execution-error model) and the Keeper Coach (minimax optimisation), together with an interactive system that communicates their outputs, and to critically evaluate the resulting recommendations.
5. To critically reflect on the system's limitations, to address its ethical dimensions of transparency, uncertainty communication and responsible use, to incorporate feedback, and to submit a thesis reporting the findings, limitations and future recommendations.

### 2.3 Contribution and Significance

The significance of this research lies in its shift from descriptive to prescriptive football analytics, and in its interdisciplinary combination of machine learning, decision theory, and game theory to address a real problem in the sport's most decisive moments. The work makes contributions on four levels.

Technical: it delivers a working system, StrikeLab, that computes calibrated xG and PSxG and, unlike existing analytics tools, prescribes optimal shot placement and goalkeeper positioning in real time from those models. The distinction from existing tools is worth stressing: the same providers who productised xG and PSxG publish descriptive outputs, but no comparable open tool composes those outputs into an explicit recommendation of where to aim or where to stand.

Methodological: it contributes two process innovations beyond the obvious. The first is an execution-error model layered over the PSxG placement sweep: because harder, more precisely aimed shots are less accurate, raw scoring probability is weighted by the probability that the shot lands on target, converting a naive "aim at the hardest-to-save spot" heuristic into a realistic expected-value optimisation. The second is a batched minimax formulation of goalkeeper positioning that evaluates the striker's best response across every candidate keeper position in a single vectorised inference pass, making the game-theoretic optimisation tractable at interactive speed. Both are concrete, reusable techniques rather than descriptions of intent.

Theoretical: by computing, rather than merely observing, the minimax-optimal goalkeeper position, the work provides a constructive counterpart to Palacios-Huerta's (2003) empirical finding that professional footballers approximate minimax play. It connects a machine-learned model of shot outcomes to the game-theoretic account of the striker-goalkeeper encounter, offering evidence about where the attacker-defender equilibrium lies for a given situation. The existing literature confirms that the equilibrium exists; this system computes where it is, given the specific geometry and shot speed of a scenario.

Ethical: it adopts a transparency-by-design approach: interpretable danger maps, explicit communication of uncertainty, and honest disclosure of the synthetic-data basis on which the models are currently trained. This operationalises responsible-AI principles as concrete design decisions rather than asserting them in the abstract.

In a broader sense, the work illustrates how machine learning can bring rigorous, decision-theoretic analysis to tactical questions that have traditionally been the preserve of intuition and elite coaching staff, doing so while foregrounding, rather than concealing, the limits of the underlying models. The aim is to promote the responsible use of AI decision-support in sport, not to replace human judgement, but to provide a transparent, well-calibrated benchmark against which it can be measured.

### 2.4 Justification of the Study

Football analytics has, over little more than a decade, transformed from a niche pursuit into a core function of professional clubs, informing recruitment worth tens of millions of pounds, opposition analysis, and in-match tactics. Expected goals and its derivatives now sit at the centre of this transformation. Yet the field's flagship metrics remain, almost without exception, retrospective: they describe the quality of chances and strikes that have already occurred. The decisive, forward-looking questions that players and coaches actually face, such as where should this shot go? and where should the goalkeeper stand?, are left to intuition. This study seeks to close that gap between measurement and decision.

At its core, a shot is a decision made under uncertainty against an intelligent opponent. The striker chooses where to aim; the goalkeeper chooses where to stand; and each is trying to outdo the other. Human agents make these choices in a fraction of a second, under physical and psychological pressure, and their choices are consequently satisficing and systematically imperfect rather than optimal. A system that can compute the normative optimum offline therefore has genuine value, not as a replacement for the player, but as a benchmark and a training aid. This motivation is not merely practical; it is grounded in expected-utility theory, game theory, and bounded rationality, as set out in the Introduction.

From a technical perspective, the study contributes to a body of research in applied machine learning and decision science. Most existing football-prediction work either focuses narrowly on improving the accuracy of a descriptive metric or treats the downstream decision as out of scope. This thesis instead composes calibrated predictive models with explicit optimisation, offering both a technical artefact and a deeper account of the strategic structure of the problem.

The justification also lies in the study's interdisciplinary relevance. The problem does not sit solely within computer science: it draws on decision theory, game theory, and sports science. Framing goalkeeper positioning as a minimax problem, and shot selection as expected-value maximisation under execution error, turns the system from a mere predictor into an instrument for reasoning about strategic interaction, with clear parallels in any adversarial decision domain.

A further justification is the timing and technological readiness of the work. Several trends have converged. Expected goals and post-shot expected goals are now established, well-understood metrics, and open event-data resources make real-world validation feasible as a next step. Mature open-source libraries, notably gradient-boosting frameworks such as XGBoost, make it possible to build, calibrate, and serve models at interactive speed without specialised infrastructure. Techniques once confined to research labs, including calibration, feature-importance analysis, and probabilistic inference, are now widely taught and applied, lowering the barrier to rigorous sports analytics. And the mainstreaming of xG in broadcast coverage and fan discourse means audiences are increasingly receptive to data-driven tactical insight.

Finally, the study holds pedagogical value: it makes abstract concepts, such as expected value, calibration, minimax equilibrium, and execution error, concrete through a system that is at once academically rigorous and immediately legible to a football audience. The same concepts that motivate the system are the ones a player, coach or student can see operating on screen.

In summary, the study is justified on several grounds: it addresses a real and unmet need, the absence of prescriptive, decision-guiding tools in a field otherwise rich in descriptive analytics; it composes calibrated machine learning with decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimisation to move from measurement to prescription; it draws on interdisciplinary foundations to provide a richer account of the strategic problem than a predictor alone; it contributes a replicable, transparent framework whose limitations, chiefly its current synthetic-data basis, are stated openly and mapped to future work; and it foregrounds the responsible use of AI decision-support, treating transparency and honesty about uncertainty as design requirements rather than afterthoughts.

### 2.5 Research Questions

Two well-scoped questions are more rigorous than several vague ones. Each research question below is answerable within the scope of the thesis, evidenced directly by the findings, and traceable through the methodology and conclusion. One is technical (RQ1) and one is ethical (RQ2); together they span both what the system can do and what it should do.

RQ1 (Technical). To what extent can machine-learning models of pre- and post-shot expected goals, combined with decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimisation, be used to prescribe optimal shot placement and goalkeeper positioning across varied shooting situations in association football?

RQ2 (Ethical). What are the ethical implications, risks, and responsibilities associated with a prescriptive football decision-support system, particularly regarding model transparency, the honest communication of uncertainty, the validity of models trained on synthetic data, and the risk of over-reliance by players and coaches, and how can these harms be identified and mitigated?

### 2.6 Research Hypotheses

Each research question has a corresponding, falsifiable hypothesis: H1 tests RQ1, and H2 tests RQ2.

Hypothesis 1 (RQ1). Gradient-boosted models trained on the spatial and contextual features of a shot, namely position, angle, goalkeeper location, and (for PSxG) placement and speed, will discriminate goals from non-goals substantially better than a geometric distance-and-angle baseline; and, composed with the minimax optimiser, will identify goalkeeper positions that measurably reduce the striker's best available scoring probability relative to naive central positioning.

Hypothesis 2 (RQ2). While the prescriptive system can improve tactical decision-making and make the reasoning behind a shot explicit, its deployment raises material ethical risks. Specifically, transparency-by-design measures, namely interpretable danger maps, explicit uncertainty display, and open disclosure of the synthetic-data basis, will mitigate the risk of misplaced trust and over-reliance among its users (players, coaches, and analysts), but full assurance of harm reduction requires validation on real data and evaluation with real users.

## 3. Research Methodology and Ethical Considerations

### 3.1 Research Methodology: An Agile, Design-and-Development Approach

This study adopts an agile, iterative design-and-development methodology to design, develop, and critically evaluate StrikeLab. The approach combines desk-based inquiry, namely a review of academic literature and an analysis of existing football-analytics metrics and tools, with the iterative construction and evaluation of a software artefact. It is well suited to the nature of the research, which involves both technical experimentation with machine-learning models and critical engagement with the ethical and contextual questions raised by a prescriptive decision-support tool in sport.

The choice of an agile framework over a linear, waterfall approach is deliberate and central to the study's rigour. A waterfall process assumes that requirements and design can be fixed at the outset; here, several of the most important design questions could only be answered by building and inspecting results. Which features would matter most to the xG model, how the post-shot model would behave, how best to communicate uncertainty, and even which prescriptive components were worth building were all questions that evolved once early prototypes were examined. An iterative, cyclical design accommodates exactly this kind of learning, allowing assumptions to be revised in response to evidence rather than committed to in advance.

At its foundation, the desk-based component of the research draws on three intersecting streams of inquiry, enabling a form of triangulation. First, academic literature provides the theoretical grounding: expected-goals and post-shot expected-goals modelling, gradient-boosted classification, goalkeeper biomechanics, and the decision-theoretic and game-theoretic principles set out in the Introduction. Second, an analysis of existing analytics tools and providers, namely the expected-goals models and event-data platforms now common in professional and public football analysis, situates the work against the current state of practice and clarifies what such tools do and, crucially, do not do. Third, technical and methodological documentation informs the engineering of the models and the prescriptive layer. Together these sources build a layered understanding of both the problem and the field.

The study's data strategy requires explicit justification, because it is also its principal limitation. The xG model is developed on a 500,000-shot professional event dataset, and the PSxG model and both prescriptive coaches are developed and demonstrated on a physics-informed synthetic dataset rather than on real match data for the post-shot phase. This choice is a considered response to a genuine constraint: the prescriptive components require, for every shot, the goalkeeper's position together with the placement and speed of the strike at the moment it is taken, synchronised information that is scarce and largely proprietary in real datasets. A synthetic generator, grounded in shot geometry and goalkeeper biomechanics (dive reach, reaction time, and the resulting save probability), makes it possible to develop and demonstrate the complete pipeline, from the two models through to the Shot and Keeper Coaches, end to end. The processing pipeline is deliberately architected so that real event data can replace the synthetic source without structural change, and the resulting caveat on external validity is treated openly throughout this thesis rather than obscured.

Each agile cycle followed the same shape: a specific research question or technical challenge was identified; the relevant data were generated and prepared; a model or feature was developed; its performance was evaluated; the results were contextualised against the literature and existing practice; and the cycle closed with a short ethical reflection. Successive sprints in this study progressed from a baseline xG model, to a feature-rich xG model, to the PSxG model, to the danger-map visualisation, to the Shot Coach with its execution-error model, to the Keeper Coach's minimax formulation, and finally to the interactive application that unifies them.

The evaluation phase of each sprint looked beyond accuracy. Because the system's outputs are probabilities that downstream components and users must be able to trust, evaluation considered both discrimination, meaning how well a model separates goals from non-goals as measured by ROC-AUC, and calibration, meaning whether a predicted probability of, say, 0.30 corresponds to an actual scoring rate near 30%, assessed with reliability diagrams and the Brier score. Accuracy alone would have been an inadequate and even misleading measure for a rare-event, probabilistic problem of this kind. Evaluation also included the qualitative sensibility of the prescriptions: whether, for example, the recommended aim point and the goalkeeper's optimal position shifted in tactically plausible ways as the scenario changed.

This connects to the study's validation strategy. Discrimination and calibration were assessed on a held-out test partition, and the prescriptive layer was probed across canonical scenarios (penalty, one-on-one, a keeper caught off his line). It must be stated plainly that, because the post-shot training data are synthetic, these procedures validate the models' internal consistency and behaviour rather than their real-world predictive validity. Empirical validation on real event data, with temporal, rather than random, splitting to avoid the leakage that would arise from training on future matches to predict past ones, is therefore identified as the priority for future work. Acknowledging this distinction is itself a methodological commitment: it names precisely what the current evaluation can and cannot establish.

In keeping with the agile philosophy, deviations from the original plan were documented and justified on the basis of evidence. Three are notable. The execution-error model was added after early testing revealed that a naive "aim where PSxG is highest" recommendation directed shots unrealistically flush against the post, ignoring the accuracy cost of extreme placement. The model-loading strategy was changed from per-request deserialisation to a cached single load once the placement sweep proved too slow otherwise. And the Keeper Coach itself emerged mid-project as the natural game-theoretic mirror of the Shot Coach, rather than being specified up front. Each change was a response to something learned by building.

Ethical reflection was integrated into the development cycle rather than reserved for a single chapter, a living-ethics approach. Decisions taken during development were repeatedly weighed for their ethical implications: the danger map was chosen over an opaque numeric score for interpretability; model uncertainty was surfaced rather than hidden; and the synthetic basis of the models was disclosed in both the interface and the documentation. These choices were guided by established principles of AI ethics, namely fairness, accountability, transparency, and respect for user autonomy, considered in Section 3.2.

The methodology was supported by a suite of open-source tools. Python (with pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, and XGBoost) was used for data generation, model training and inference; FastAPI served the models as a JSON API; and a React, TypeScript and Next.js client provided the interactive interface. Version control was managed with Git. These tools align with the agile philosophy in supporting rapid iteration, reproducibility, and a clean separation between the modelling and presentation layers.

The methodology is not without limitations. The reliance on synthetic data for the post-shot phase, while justified above, means the models' real-world validity is not established by this work. The ethical analysis, though systematic, is reasoned rather than empirically validated through user or stakeholder study. And the game-theoretic model deliberately simplifies the encounter, treating goalkeeper positioning as a one-dimensional lateral choice and modelling execution error with independent Gaussian spread; these simplifications make the problem tractable and interpretable but abstract away aspects of a real striker-goalkeeper interaction. Each limitation is revisited in the Conclusion and mapped to future work.

The agile, design-and-development methodology adopted in this thesis supports a flexible yet rigorous investigation of prescriptive shot analytics. It keeps the research grounded in evidence, iterative in response to what each sprint revealed, and ethically aware throughout, qualities that are necessary for the responsible development of decision-support systems, even in a domain as apparently low-stakes as sport.

### 3.2 Ethical Considerations

A prescriptive decision-support system carries responsibilities that a descriptive metric does not. Even though StrikeLab uses no personal data and concerns sport rather than a safety-critical domain such as healthcare or finance, the fact that it advises, and that it currently does so from models trained partly on synthetic data, raises ethical questions that must be addressed systematically rather than dismissed with a disclaimer. This section analyses four harm pathways, each paired with the design response taken in the system, and situates them within established ethical frameworks.

Direct user harm: over-reliance and false authority. The most immediate risk of a prescriptive tool is that its users defer to it. A system that presents an "optimal" aim point or goalkeeper position with apparent scientific authority can encourage overconfidence, leading coaches, analysts or players to trust it even when it is wrong, and, because the present models are trained partly on synthetic data, it may indeed be wrong about real match dynamics. This risk of deskilling and misplaced trust is the domain-specific analogue of the over-reliance that predictive systems create in higher-stakes settings. The design response is to present every recommendation alongside its uncertainty, to frame outputs explicitly as decision-support benchmarks rather than instructions, and to disclose the models' data basis wherever results are shown.

Systemic and competitive exploitation. A tool built for analysis and coaching could be repurposed against its intent. The same engine that recommends where to shoot could, for example, be adapted by gambling operators to estimate in-play scoring probabilities; and more subtly, sophisticated analytics can widen the gap between wealthy clubs and resource-poor ones, intensifying an arms race that disadvantages the latter. The response adopted here is a commitment to transparency and an open, documented methodology aimed at democratising rather than concentrating analytical capability, together with an explicit statement that the system's intended use is analytical, educational, and coaching support.

Data privacy and consent. For the system as built, this risk is eliminated by design: the synthetic dataset contains no real player, personal, or behavioural data, so there is no data subject whose privacy or consent is at issue. This is a categorically stronger position than mitigating a privacy risk by policy. It is important to be honest, however, that the intended extension to real event and tracking data would reintroduce this concern, since player-performance data is personal data, subject to regimes such as the GDPR and to questions of player consent, and the thesis therefore flags responsible handling of such data as a precondition for any real-data deployment.

Opacity and accountability. Machine-learning models can function as "black boxes" that produce outputs without intelligible reasoning, eroding users' ability to challenge, learn from, or hold the system accountable. This concern is well documented in the wider literature: Lipton (2018) distinguishes the distinct goals bundled under "interpretability", and Rudin (2019) argues that for high-stakes decisions, an interpretable model should be used in place of a black box whose predictions are merely explained after the fact. StrikeLab's response is architectural: the goal-frame danger map renders the PSxG surface visible rather than reducing it to a single opaque number; feature-importance analysis exposes what drives the models; uncertainty is communicated explicitly; and the synthetic-data basis is openly disclosed. Transparency here is a design property of the system, not a documentation exercise appended to it.

Underlying all four pathways is a concern specific to this project: the honest communication of the models' data basis. To present outputs trained on synthetic data as though they were empirically validated on real football would be a form of misrepresentation. The mitigation is clear and repeated disclosure, in the interface, in the documentation, and in this thesis, and the treatment of real-data validation as required future work rather than as an optional extra.

These responses are informed by established frameworks, applied as concrete design criteria rather than cited as background. The transparency and accountability principles of the IEEE's Ethically Aligned Design and the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence directly motivate the interpretability and uncertainty-communication choices above; the EU AI Act, under which a system of this kind would not be classified as high-risk, nonetheless supplies transparency expectations that the design adopts voluntarily; and the GDPR frames the data-governance obligations that any future real-data version would need to meet. The distinction between interpretability and explainability drawn in this literature is one the thesis takes seriously: the system is designed so that the basis of a recommendation is inspectable, rather than relying on post-hoc narrative explanations of an opaque prediction.

## 4. Literature Review

### 4.1 Introduction to the Review

This chapter reviews the literature on three planes: technical, theoretical, and practical. The technical plane covers the machine-learning foundations of shot-outcome modelling, including expected goals, post-shot expected goals, gradient boosting, and model evaluation and calibration. The theoretical plane covers the decision-theoretic and game-theoretic principles, namely expected utility, minimax, and bounded rationality, that motivate prescribing rather than merely measuring. The practical plane covers the state of applied football analytics, its gap with respect to prescription, and the open challenges in data and validation that shape this work. The review closes by stating the gap this thesis addresses and the justification for the approach.

The review is necessarily selective. Shot analytics has grown into a large and fast-moving literature, and the aim here is not encyclopedic coverage but a structured account of the concepts the thesis builds on, the tools it uses, and the space it enters. Each source is chosen because it carries weight in the argument; where a claim rests on the author's own engineering judgment rather than a published result, that is stated explicitly.

### 4.2 Technical Foundations: Expected Goals and Post-Shot Expected Goals

Expected goals is the probability that a shot results in a goal, estimated from the shot's context. The concept has a long informal history, but it became a structured and widely adopted practice through the work of analysts and providers such as Opta and StatsBomb, and through academic treatments including Rathke (2017), who formalised xG estimation using logistic regression on shot features, and the more recent practice-oriented literature of Green (2012), who described the fundamentals of a range of football analytics metrics, and associated works documenting the wide variety of commercial xG models. The core idea is simple and powerful: instead of counting goals and shots, model the quality of each chance, so that teams and players can be compared on chances created and conceded independently of the noise of individual goals. An xG of 0.5 for a shot says that half of comparable shots would score; a striker's season xG total sums these probabilities to estimate the goals an average finisher would have scored from the chances received, separating chance quality from finishing skill.

The strength of xG is that it is a calibrated probability rather than a heuristic index, which makes it composable: probabilities from individual shots aggregate into expected totals across matches and seasons. Its principal weakness is its coarse granularity. Most published xG models are trained on event data that records the shot's location, the assist, and some contextual features, but the goalkeeper's position and the placement and speed of the strike are usually unavailable, so the model cannot distinguish a shot that would beat most goalkeepers from one aimed straight at them. This is precisely the information gap that post-shot expected goals addresses.

Post-shot expected goals (PSxG) conditions on the ball having reached the goal frame and estimates the scoring probability from the placement and quality of the strike rather than from the chance that preceded it. Whereas xG asks "how good was the chance?", PSxG asks "how good was the shot?", given where it went and how it was struck. Because PSxG conditions on the shot reaching the goal, the model's task shifts from the whole shot event to the geometry and quality of the strike itself: the zone of the goal it entered, its speed, and the goalkeeper's position and ability to react. Commercial implementations vary, but the underlying concept is consistent, and it is the natural model for evaluating both finishing and goalkeeping, separating the goalkeeper's contribution from the chance the defence conceded. The distinction is a real one: a central shot that an xG model rates at 0.30 may carry a PSxG of 0.80 if it is rifled into the top corner with no keeper in reach, and the difference between the two numbers, xG minus PSxG for attackers, PSxG minus goals for goalkeepers, is precisely where finishing and shot-stopping live.

A further generation of models extends the same logic beyond the shot. The possession-value framework of Fernandez, Bornn and Cervone (2018) assigns value to every state of play by modelling how possession states evolve into shots, allowing any action to be valued by how it changes the team's expected scoring probability. Decroos, Bransen, Van Haaren and Davis (2019) proposed VAEP, a value of actions in event space that scores each action by the change in the team's probability of scoring or conceding, and showed it is well correlated with player valuations. These models represent the state of the art in descriptive football analytics: they measure the value of actions, but they do not prescribe which action to take, which is the gap this thesis addresses. The distinction deserves emphasis: a value model can tell you, after the fact, that a given pass was worth 0.05 expected goals, but it does not, at the point of decision, tell the player which of the available passes or shots maximises that value. That forward-looking task requires composing the value model with an optimisation over the decision space, which is the project undertaken here.

### 4.3 Technical Foundations: Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees

The choice of learning algorithm is not incidental; it is forced by the nature of the data. Shot-outcome data are tabular, with strong non-linear interactions between continuous spatial features and categorical context, and the outcome is a rare event, roughly one goal in nine or ten shots. These properties, tabular, feature-dense, non-linear and imbalanced, are precisely those for which gradient-boosted decision trees are the state of the art.

Gradient boosting, as formalised by Freund and Schapire (1997) in AdaBoost and generalised by Friedman (2001) in his statistical view of boosting, builds an ensemble of shallow decision trees sequentially: each new tree is fitted to the residuals of the ensemble so far, in the direction of steepest descent on a differentiable loss, so that the model progressively corrects its own errors. The sequential, additive structure gives boosting a decisive advantage over a single tree (whose capacity is limited) and over bagged ensembles such as random forests (which build trees independently and cannot correct each other's errors).

XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) is a highly engineered implementation of gradient boosting that has become the default tool for tabular learning. Its success rests on a combination of practical features: a regularised objective that penalises model complexity and reduces overfitting; efficient, cache-aware computation of the splitting criterion; native handling of missing values; and a column-block structure that allows parallel training. It is also, at inference time, fast enough for interactive use, which matters directly for this project, since the Shot and Keeper Coaches query the PSxG model across a grid of candidate aim points and keeper positions and must return an answer at interactive speed.

There is a well-documented trade-off. Boosting's power comes at the price of interpretability: an ensemble of hundreds of shallow trees cannot be read as a single rule or a set of coefficients. The practical consequence is a two-stage approach: the model's behaviour is made legible through feature-importance analysis (which identifies the features that most drive predictions) and through the system's visualisations, such as the danger map, which renders the model's outputs directly rather than requiring the user to read a number. This is a deliberate design decision examined further in the Technical Development and Findings chapters. The modelling literature is clear that importance measures are indicative rather than causal, and the thesis treats them accordingly, as diagnostics rather than explanations of footballing causality.

### 4.4 Technical Foundations: Evaluation, Calibration, and the Rare-Event Problem

Evaluating a rare-event probabilistic model is a subject in itself, and this section sets out the commitments the thesis makes, because the choice of evaluation strategy determines what the findings can claim.

The first commitment concerns discrimination: how well does the model separate goals from non-goals? The standard tool is the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and its area under the curve (AUC), which summarises, across all possible thresholds, the trade-off between the true-positive and false-positive rates. AUC is threshold-independent and unaffected by class imbalance in a way that accuracy is not; a model that predicts "no goal" for everything would still score high accuracy on a dataset with a 10% goal rate, and AUC correctly identifies such a model as useless. The thesis therefore reports AUC alongside any accuracy figure. AUC does, however, measure ordering only, not the truthfulness of the probability values themselves, which motivates the second commitment.

The second commitment concerns calibration: is a predicted probability of 0.30 reflected in an actual scoring rate near 30%? The standard tools are the reliability diagram, which plots predicted probability against observed frequency in bins, and the Brier score (Brier, 1950), the mean squared error between predicted probabilities and binary outcomes, which simultaneously penalises miscalibration and weakness of discrimination. A model can have high AUC and poor calibration, and for a system whose probabilities feed downstream optimisation, calibration matters as much as discrimination: the Shot Coach's expected-value calculation is only as sound as the probabilities it multiplies. Platt scaling (Platt, 1999) and isotonic regression are the standard recalibration methods; the thesis reports calibration metrics and applies recalibration where needed, and the Findings chapter discusses what the calibration results show. The thesis also engages with the distinction, prominent in Guo, Pleiss, Sun and Weinberger (2017), between different notions of calibration in modern classification, and states clearly which notion is being reported.

The third commitment concerns how the data are split. For a time-series problem such as football, random splitting risks leakage: training on future matches to predict past ones, which inflates apparent performance. Temporal splitting, training on an early period and validating on a later one, is the honest choice, and the thesis identifies it as the required standard for the real-data validation that is identified as future work. The same logic applies, mutatis mutandis, to the synthetic PSxG dataset: the generator is a deterministic rule with a random component, and the model must not merely memorise the specific random draws. The synthetic set was therefore generated once, split into train and hold-out partitions, and the model's fidelity to the generator's rule was measured on the hold-out partition it had never seen, which is what makes the reported AUC of 0.901 a statement about generalisation to unseen samples of the same rule rather than about memorisation.

A further commitment concerns the meaning of the numbers themselves, and it is worth stating because it recurs throughout the thesis. An AUC of 0.83 does not mean that a striker's individual shots can be predicted; it means that the model reliably orders chances by quality. The two claims are frequently confused in public discussion of predictive models, and the confusion matters here because the prescriptive layer depends on the ordering and the expected values, not on the prediction of individual goals. The thesis reports its evaluation metrics precisely so that the reader can see what each claim does and does not establish: the AUC figures establish ordering ability; the Brier figures establish probability truthfulness; neither establishes the ability to predict any single shot, and no honest evaluation of a rare-event model would claim that it could.

### 4.5 Theoretical Foundations: Expected Utility, Minimax, and Bounded Rationality

A prescriptive shot-analytics system is grounded in three theoretical ideas, each of which was introduced in the Introduction and is developed here in relation to the literature. These ideas are not decorative; they are the formal content of the system's prescriptive layer.

The first is expected-utility theory (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944), which provides the normative standard for decisions under uncertainty: choose the action that maximises expected utility, the probability-weighted average of the utility of each outcome. For a striker, the relevant utility is the probability of scoring, and the prescription is to aim where the expected probability of a goal is greatest, accounting for the probability that the shot reaches the target at all. The Shot Coach is this principle operationalised. The value of the framework is that it forces a correct treatment of uncertainty: the shot with the highest probability of scoring if on target is not necessarily the shot with the highest probability of scoring overall, because the two must be multiplied by the probability of being on target, and expected-utility reasoning is what makes the multiplication explicit rather than left to intuition.

The second is game theory and its minimax principle (von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944; Nash, 1950). The striker and goalkeeper face directly opposed objectives, so the encounter is a zero-sum game: any gain for one is a loss for the other. In such games, the minimax principle prescribes that each player should choose the strategy that maximises the payoff from the worst-case response of the opponent, and the minimax theorem guarantees that this yields a well-defined equilibrium, the value of the game, provided mixed strategies are allowed. In this thesis, the striker's strategy set is the space of aim points, the goalkeeper's is the space of positions, and the payoff is the expected probability of scoring. The Keeper Coach computes the goalkeeper position that minimises the striker's best response, which is a direct application of minimax reasoning, and reports the resulting value of the game as the scoring probability the striker can guarantee at best, against the goalkeeper's optimal position.

The connection to football is not merely formal; it is empirically grounded. Palacios-Huerta (2003) analysed several hundred penalty kicks in top European leagues and found that players' shot directions and goalkeepers' dive directions closely matched the mixed-strategy minimax equilibrium, one of the strongest real-world confirmations of minimax play in the literature. Walker and Wooders (2001) reached the same conclusion for professional tennis service directions, and Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) confirmed the penalty-kick result while explicitly accommodating player heterogeneity. These findings have a double significance for this thesis: they establish that minimax equilibrium is not an idealisation but a description of expert behaviour under pressure, and they imply that a prescriptive tool computing the same equilibrium, at a fine spatial granularity the penalty literature never examined, is providing something experts already approximate, which makes the tool's recommendations credible and useful, while also raising the question of whether and how much an explicit recommendation could improve on expert intuition.

The third is bounded rationality (Simon, 1955), which explains why such a tool is needed at all. Simon's central claim is that real decision-makers cannot enumerate all options and compute the optimum; their cognitive and temporal resources are limited, so they satisfice, choosing options that are good enough rather than optimal. The striker deciding where to aim in the instant before striking has no time for exhaustive optimisation, and the goalkeeper even less. The prescriptive system does not replace this judgment; it computes offline what the player cannot compute online, and provides a benchmark for training and reflection. The distinction between the normative optimum (what the system computes) and the descriptive actual (what players do) is the space in which the system's value, and its ethical limits, reside.

### 4.6 The Practice of Football Analytics Today

The practical context of this thesis is the rapid professionalisation of football analytics over the past decade. What began as a niche pursuit in a handful of clubs is now an industry standard: analytics departments are embedded in most professional clubs, and analytics companies supply event data, tracking data, and model-based insights to clubs, broadcasters, and the public. The flagship products of this industry are descriptive metrics, and among them xG and its derivatives are the most prominent, appearing in live broadcast graphics and in the everyday vocabulary of fans and pundits.

The commercial reality of football data is a decisive constraint on research in this area. High-quality event and tracking data are expensive and governed by restrictive licences; synchronised tracking of the goalkeeper's position at the moment of each shot, the information the prescriptive layer needs, is scarcer still. The consequences for research are twofold. First, much of the open literature is built on limited or older datasets, which limits the generalisability of published findings. Second, research on the prescriptive use of analytics, which is exactly what this thesis attempts, is constrained by data availability in a way that descriptive work is not, because prescription requires the granular, synchronised data that is the most tightly held. This is the practical reason, stated honestly, why the post-shot model and the prescriptive layer of this system are demonstrated on synthetic data: the information needed, the goalkeeper's position and the strike's placement and speed, is precisely what is hardest to obtain. The methodological decision is revisited throughout the thesis.

A further practical consideration is the audience. The consumers of these models are not only analysts; they include players, coaches, broadcasters, and, increasingly, a general public that follows xG as part of following football. This audience is receptive to data-driven tactical insight, which lowers the barrier to adoption of a prescriptive tool, but it also magnifies the responsibility to present outputs honestly. A viewer who has come to trust the xG number on screen will readily extend that trust to a shot-placement recommendation, and the system's transparency-by-design commitments, the danger map, the uncertainty display, and the disclosed data basis, are partly a response to this receptivity.

A further practical consideration is the diversity of the models themselves. There is no single canonical xG; every provider publishes its own variant, and the same shot will receive different values from different vendors, a fact that is well known in practice and often unremarked in the literature. The consequence is that the field's flagship number is not a single measurement but a family of measurements, each with its own feature set, training data and calibration, and comparisons across providers are therefore comparisons of different instruments. This thesis takes no side in that competition: it builds its own explicit, documented model, reports its own features and its own figures, and treats the divergence between providers as evidence that the field's metrics are models, with premises and limits, rather than measurements of a fixed quantity. That stance, that the metric is a model with disclosed premises, runs through the entire system and is one of the practical commitments the thesis makes explicit.

### 4.7 The Gap: From Measurement to Prescription

The preceding sections establish the state of the field: technically sophisticated, descriptively powerful, and theoretically groundable in decision and game theory, but almost uniformly retrospective. This section states the gap the thesis addresses, and does so precisely.

Descriptive analytics, xG, PSxG, possession-value models and their relatives, answer questions about what has already happened: how good was this chance, how well was this shot struck, how much did this pass change the team's scoring probability. These are evaluative questions, and they are answered by models that predict the expected outcome of a past event. Prescriptive analytics answers a different question: given the situation, what should the agent do? For a striker: where should this shot go? For a goalkeeper: where should I stand? The second question cannot be answered by a predictor alone; it requires composing a predictive model with an optimisation over the decision space. This composition, a calibrated shot-outcome model embedded in a decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimiser, is precisely what is missing from the literature and from the available tools.

There are partial precedents. The penalty-kick literature, Palacios-Huerta (2003) and Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002), establishes that expert shot choices approximate a minimax equilibrium, but it studies behaviour, not prescription, and it is confined to the fixed geometry of the penalty kick. The wider analytics literature, and the commercial tools it has produced, stops at measurement. No comparable open system, to the author's knowledge, composes calibrated xG and PSxG models with an expected-value and minimax optimiser to recommend, at interactive speed and in a form a coach can use, where to aim and where to stand. This thesis builds precisely such a system.

The gap is not only practical but theoretical. The penalty literature measures how closely behaviour matches an equilibrium; it does not compute where the equilibrium lies for an arbitrary situation, which requires a calibrated model of the shot's scoring probability as a function of aim point and keeper position. By providing that model and computing the minimax position, this thesis supplies the constructive counterpart to the empirical finding: it can locate the equilibrium for any given geometry and shot speed, and ask whether the penalty-kick result generalises beyond the fixed penalty geometry to open play.

Finally, the gap includes a methodological commitment. A prescriptive tool trained and demonstrated on synthetic data must be honest about what it does and does not claim, and the thesis treats the validation of the prescriptive layer on real data as a necessary next step rather than a desirable extra. The review of the literature supports this framing: the field's own validation practice, when it is conducted at all, has been improved by the recent emphasis on honest evaluation, and this thesis follows that practice by separating what the current evidence establishes from what it does not.

### 4.8 Summary of the Review and Justification

The literature review has established three things. Technically, shot-outcome modelling is a tabular, non-linear, imbalanced classification problem, for which gradient-boosted decision trees are the appropriate tool, and for which evaluation must address both discrimination and calibration rather than accuracy alone. Theoretically, the shot encounter is a decision under uncertainty against an adversarial opponent, whose normative solution, expected-value maximisation within a minimax equilibrium, is grounded in decision and game theory, and whose real-world relevance is established by the penalty-kick and tennis literature demonstrating that experts approximate minimax play. Practically, the football-analytics industry has productised descriptive metrics while leaving the prescriptive task, what to do, unserved, and the granular data the prescriptive task requires is scarce and proprietary.

The gap is therefore clear and well-founded: a calibrated, interactive, prescriptive shot-analytics system, composed of xG and PSxG models with expected-value and minimax optimisation, does not exist as an open, working system, and its construction is both technically feasible and theoretically motivated. The next chapter describes the development of that system in detail.

## 5. Technical Development

### 5.1 Development Overview and Tooling

This chapter documents the technical development of StrikeLab: the models, the prescriptive engines, the data pipeline, and the interactive application, together with the problems encountered along the way and the corrections made in response. It is written to be reproducible and, where a decision was a judgment call rather than a consequence, to say so.

The system was developed in Python 3.11 with a standard scientific stack. Pandas and NumPy handled data preparation and batch computation; XGBoost (Chen and Guestrin, 2016) provided the gradient-boosted classifiers; scikit-learn supplied train-test splitting, cross-validation helpers, and evaluation metrics including ROC-AUC and the Brier score. The interactive client was built as a Next.js application with React and TypeScript, using Tailwind CSS for styling. The models were served through a FastAPI application exposing a small JSON API, which the client called at runtime. Version control was managed with Git.

The architecture separates three concerns. The modelling layer consists of two trained classifiers, the pre-shot xG model and the post-shot PSxG model, each a serialised XGBoost model with a companion JSON of calibration factors and inference constants. The prescriptive layer consists of two engine modules, the Shot Coach and the Keeper Coach, which call the PSxG model in batched inference. The presentation layer is the web application, which renders the models' outputs as interactive visualisations and exposes the prescriptive tools. This separation was deliberate: it lets the models be trained, re-trained, and evaluated independently of the user-facing system, and it lets the prescriptive engines be tested as pure computation.

The development proceeded in the iterative cycles described in Chapter 3. This chapter reports the finished system and, along the way, the principal problems that shaped it: the class-imbalance of the real data, the need for an execution-error model, the size and speed of the placement sweep, and the calibration of the raw model outputs.

### 5.2 The Pre-Shot Expected-Goals Model

The xG model estimates the probability that a shot results in a goal, from the shot's context. It is trained on 500,000 real professional shots, of which 47,576 were goals, a goal rate of 9.52%, which is close to the rate reported in the wider literature for top-level football (Perez-Arroniz et al., 2025). The imbalance is severe and central: a model that predicted "no goal" for every shot would be 90.5% accurate and entirely useless, which is why evaluation rests on ROC-AUC and calibration rather than accuracy.

The feature space is deliberately rich and organised into eleven feature groups, ninety-nine features in total, spanning the geometry of the shot, the configuration of play, and the context of the match. It is worth enumerating the groups precisely, both because they define what the model can see and because they are a compact summary of what a coach considers relevant at the moment of a shot. The eleven groups are: the shooter's position and distance from goal; the angle available at the shot, which is the angle subtended by the goal frame from the shooter's location and captures whether the shot has a wide or narrow target; the goalkeeper's position and how far off his line he stands, which determines how much of the goal is exposed; the number and position of defending players between the shooter and goal, which captures the blocking of shooting lanes; the width and lateral location of the shot relative to the goal frame; the assist type and preceding action that created the chance, such as a through ball or a cut-back; the body part used, which sharply separates headed chances from struck ones; the match situation and scoreline, which influence risk-taking; the phase of play, namely open play, set piece, counter-attack, penalty and through ball; the shot's placement in the goal frame for those shots that reached it; and a final group of derived spatial interactions, such as the relationship between the shooter's distance and the goalkeeper's depth, which capture effects that no single feature expresses alone. Each group was included on footballing grounds before any feature-selection procedure was applied, on the argument that the model should be given the information a coach would consider relevant, and left to learn which combinations matter. The ninety-nine features include both the raw values and a substantial number of derived ratios and differences, because the relevant signal in shot data is frequently relational, for example the gap between the shot placement and the keeper's reach, rather than a set of independent magnitudes.

The model is an XGBoost classifier trained with 800 boosting rounds and a maximum tree depth of 12, a learning rate of 0.02, gamma of 2.0, L1 regularisation alpha of 0.25, L2 regularisation lambda of 2.5, and a minimum child weight of 35. These hyperparameters were chosen through a combination of grid search and judgement: depth and regularisation balance capacity against overfitting on a dataset this size, and the low learning rate with many rounds favours a smooth, well-converged boosting path. The choice of XGBoost itself was argued in Chapter 4: tabular, non-linear, imbalanced data is exactly the regime where gradient-boosted trees are state of the art.

The evaluation followed the two-axis strategy set out in Chapter 4. On discrimination, the held-out test set yielded an ROC-AUC of 0.830, comfortably above the geometric distance-and-angle baseline and consistent with the performance of published xG models on comparable data. On calibration, the raw model probabilities showed a modest miscalibration, a mild overconfidence pattern typical of boosted models, which was corrected with a piecewise calibration derived from observed scoring rates in probability bins; after correction, the calibration error dropped materially and the Brier score on the test set was approximately 0.076. These figures are reported as the system's own results in Chapter 6, alongside the caveat that the Brier and AUC figures quoted from the development run are reproduced from the system's logs and require a clean re-run to be certified independently.

A consequence of the low goal rate deserves explicit discussion. Even at an AUC of 0.83, a single shot's goal probability is small almost everywhere, and the model's value is not in predicting individual goals but in estimating chance quality precisely enough to aggregate across shots and to rank options within a situation. This distinction, between the value of a calibrated probability and the impossibility of certain prediction, is carried through the design of the prescriptive layer.

### 5.3 The Post-Shot Expected-Goals Model and the Synthetic Data Problem

The PSxG model estimates the probability that a shot that reaches the goal frame scores, given the placement and quality of the strike and the goalkeeper's ability to reach it. This is the model the prescriptive engines need, because the Shot Coach's question, where should the shot be aimed?, is exactly a question about placement on target.

The data problem, stated honestly and repeatedly in this thesis, is that the information this model requires, the goalkeeper's position at the moment of the shot and the strike's placement and speed, is the scarcest and most proprietary information in football data. Real events of this granularity are not available to this project. The PSxG model is therefore trained on a physics-informed synthetic dataset of one million shots, generated from shot geometry and goalkeeper biomechanics rather than from observed match events.

The generator is the heart of this design and deserves a precise description. Each synthetic shot is constructed from four physical quantities: the speed of the strike, the distance from which it is taken, the lateral position from which it is struck, and the placement of the shot within the goal frame. These are sampled from distributions grounded in the footballing range of each quantity, and the distributions were deliberately chosen to reproduce, in shape rather than merely in bounds, the way real shots are distributed. Shot speed is drawn from a mixture of four normal components centred at 55, 75, 95 and 115 km/h with standard deviations of 8, 10, 8 and 10 respectively, reflecting that real strikes cluster around a few archetypes, a driven effort, a placed shot, a struck ball and a rifled one, and the samples are clipped to the physical range of 40 to 130 km/h, below which a shot is hardly a shot and above which human striking is not observed. Distance is drawn from an exponential distribution with a rate parameter of 12, shifted by 5 metres and clipped to the range 5 to 40 metres, which reproduces the well-known fact that most shots are taken close to goal and that the frequency of shots falls off steeply with distance. Lateral position is spread across the width of the attacking third, and the placement of the shot within the goal frame is drawn from a four-way split, 25% of placements aimed at the lower zones, 35% at the middle, 30% at the upper zones and 10% at the top corners, a distribution that encodes the observation that strikers aim for the corners and the lower frame far more often than the centre. Each shot is then assigned a goalkeeper position, with the keeper's lateral position sampled from a normal distribution centred on the goal's midline with a standard deviation of 0.8 metres, reflecting that a keeper is rarely perfectly central but usually near it, and clipped to the width of the goal.

These numbers are reported exactly because they are the definition of the synthetic world the post-shot model inhabits, and because any future user of the system needs to know them to judge the model. They are also the clearest illustration of the thesis's central caveat: the generator is grounded in the physical ranges of real football, but the mixture weights and the placement split are engineering choices, informed by footballing intuition rather than measured from real data. If real event and tracking data later become available, the same generator structure can be refitted to the empirical distributions; the architecture anticipates that substitution, which is why it is described here in this much detail.

The scoring probability is computed from goalkeeper biomechanics, and the physical constants deserve to be stated exactly because they are what the model means by "save". The goalkeeper is modelled with a lateral dive speed of 4.0 metres per second, a reaction time of 0.15 seconds, a diving reach of 0.75 metres beyond his standing position, and a standing reach of 2.30 metres. Given the shot's placement, the time the ball takes to reach the goal line is computed from the shot's distance and speed by simple kinematics; the distance the keeper must travel is computed from the placement and the keeper's position; and the keeper saves the shot if the required travel distance is within his reachable envelope, that is, within the reach his dive speed, reaction time and reach allow in the time available. These constants are not arbitrary: the reaction time of 0.15 seconds and the dive speed of around 4 metres per second are in line with published sports-science values for elite goalkeepers, and the reaches correspond to the proportions of a goalkeeper of typical professional height relative to the 7.32-metre goal. The result is a deterministic, physical rule that decides each synthetic shot, and the goal rate it produces, 45.85% of the one million synthetic shots, is the proportion of strikes the biomechanical keeper can reach and save, far higher than real-world scoring because the synthetic shots are, by construction, on target, which is what PSxG conditions on. The rule is a simplification of real goalkeeping, but it is a defensible one: it captures the geometry and timing that dominate real goalkeeper saves, and it makes the model's physics explicit and inspectable. The same constants are used, unchanged, by the Shot Coach and the Keeper Coach, so that the prescriptive layer and the model inhabit the same world.

The PSxG model itself is a second XGBoost classifier, trained on the one million synthetic samples with 27 features, thirteen base physical quantities and fourteen derived ones, and 200 boosting rounds with a maximum depth of 8. The feature set includes the shot's speed, distance, lateral position, and placement zone; the goalkeeper's position and displacement; the derived flight time, travel distance, and required dive reach; and the interaction terms, such as the difference between required and available reach, that the biomechanical rule depends on. The model is trained to reproduce the physical rule, and its evaluation must be read with that in mind: on the held-out synthetic test set it reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.901, and it is well calibrated, but this measures how faithfully it learns the generator's physical rule, not how well it predicts real-world outcomes. The distinction is drawn sharply in the Findings chapter and is the central caveat of this thesis.

The 27-feature, 8-depth, 200-round design was chosen by experimentation: the model needed enough capacity to reproduce the biomechanical rule but not so much that it overfit the generator's specific noise structure, and 200 rounds at depth 8 proved sufficient to drive the AUC above 0.90 while remaining fast enough for the batched inference the prescriptive layer requires.

### 5.4 The Prescriptive Layer: the Shot Coach

The Shot Coach answers the striker's question: given the situation, where should I aim? Its output is an expected-value recommendation over the space of aim points within the goal frame.

The naive approach would be to evaluate the PSxG model over a grid of aim points and recommend the point of maximum scoring probability. Early testing showed this to be wrong in a specific and instructive way: the recommendation consistently directed shots unrealistically flush against the post and the crossbar, where the PSxG model, on target, says the keeper is beaten, ignoring the fact that such extreme placements are rarely achieved. The correction is the execution-error model, and it is the intellectual core of the Shot Coach.

The execution-error model represents the fact that a striker aiming at a point does not hit that point exactly; the strike's actual placement is a sample from a distribution centred on the aim point, with spread that grows with the difficulty of the placement. In the current implementation the spread is modelled as Gaussian in the plane of the goal, with a standard deviation that scales from roughly 0.22 metres for a simple placement to roughly 0.42 metres for an extreme one, reflecting the empirical intuition that harder, more precisely placed shots are less consistently accurate. The strike's distance from the aim point degrades the expected scoring value in two ways: it may move the ball off target altogether, and it may move it into a zone the keeper can reach.

The Shot Coach's calculation is then a genuine expected-value maximisation. For each candidate aim point on a 91-point grid spanning the goal frame, it samples the execution-error distribution, evaluates the resulting placement distribution through the PSxG model, and weights each placement by the probability of hitting it. The recommended aim point is the one of greatest expected scoring value, and the expected-value logic is exactly that of the utility theory set out in Chapter 4: the naive recommendation multiplies only the probability of scoring given on target by its (wrongly assumed) certainty, while the corrected one multiplies the probability of scoring given the realised placement by the probability of achieving that placement. The numerical difference between the two recommendations, and the physical difference between aiming into the corner versus aiming just inside the post, is the practical pay-off of the execution-error model.

A further design detail shows the model's behaviour is sanity-checked. Shots taken from very close range, or into an empty net, have their scoring probabilities clamped to a floor just below 1.0, on the grounds that an open-goal tap-in is near-certain but not literally certain, and shots taken from beyond a threshold distance are subject to a long-range override that slightly depresses their value, reflecting that very long shots, however well placed, score less often in real data than the synthetic model would imply. Penalty kicks, which the PSxG model was not designed to handle, fall back to a fixed, published penalty conversion rate. These rules are small but important: they encode footballing priors that the synthetic training data cannot supply, and they are disclosed as such.

### 5.5 The Prescriptive Layer: the Keeper Coach

The Keeper Coach answers the goalkeeper's question: given the situation, where should I stand? Its formulation is game-theoretic, and it is the constructive counterpart to the penalty-kick literature discussed in Chapter 4.

The encounter is modelled as a zero-sum game. The striker's strategy set is the space of aim points; the goalkeeper's strategy set is the space of positions along the goal line; and the payoff is the expected probability of scoring, which the PSxG model computes as a function of both the aim point and the keeper's position. The striker wants to maximise the payoff, the goalkeeper to minimise it. The minimax solution is the goalkeeper position that minimises the striker's best available aim point: the position from which, even if the striker chooses optimally, the scoring probability is as low as it can be forced.

The computational problem is that a naive evaluation would require, for every candidate keeper position, a separate sweep over the striker's aim-point space, and the resulting two-dimensional search is slow. The solution adopted here is batched inference: the PSxG model is evaluated in a single vectorised pass over the full product grid of candidate aim points and candidate keeper positions, and the Keeper Coach then scans the resulting payoff matrix, reading off, for each keeper position, the striker's best aim point, and selecting the keeper position for which that best response is minimised. This turns a nested search into a single model call followed by a simple table scan, and it is what makes the minimax optimisation feasible at interactive speed. The technique, replacing a nested computation by a batched outer product and a scan, is one of the thesis's methodological contributions, and it is described in Chapter 6 as such.

The Keeper Coach's output is tripartite and deliberately informative rather than a bare number. It reports the recommended goalkeeper position; the striker's best aim point against that position, with the corresponding scoring probability, which is the value of the game; and, where useful, the striker's best aim point against the naive central position, so that the user sees what the recommendation actually buys. In canonical scenarios the outputs are tactically sensible: against a central position the striker's best option is into a corner, and the Keeper Coach's recommendation shifts the keeper toward the near post as the striker's position and angle change, in ways that match coaching intuition. The Findings chapter probes these scenarios and reports the numbers.

The connection to the penalty literature is direct and worth making. Palacios-Huerta (2003) and Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) showed that professional strikers and keepers at penalties approximate a minimax equilibrium; the Keeper Coach computes, for an arbitrary open-play geometry and shot speed, where that equilibrium lies. The system therefore does not merely apply game theory to football; it operationalises the empirical result of the penalty literature at a spatial granularity and in situations the literature never examined.

### 5.6 The Interactive Application and Its Visualisations

The system's value depends on its outputs being legible to a coach or player who is not a data scientist. The presentation layer was therefore designed around two interactive visualisations, the danger map and the shot corridor, and around the two coaches exposed as tools.

The danger map renders the PSxG model's output as a heatmap over the goal frame: the colour at each point of the goal is the scoring probability of a shot placed there, given the current goalkeeper position and shot scenario, with a twelve-zone grid over the goal frame providing a coarse, coach-friendly summary on top of the continuous map. The choice of a map over a bare number was a deliberate transparency decision, discussed in Chapter 3: the map renders the model's reasoning visible, so the user sees why a recommendation is what it is, rather than receiving an opaque verdict.

A second map overlays the pre-shot model's 25-zone grid over the shooting half, colouring the pitch by the xG of a shot taken from each zone, so the user sees how chance quality varies across the pitch and how the coaches' recommendations relate to it. The two maps, the placement-based one over the goal frame and the position-based one over the pitch, are the two questions the system answers, where to aim and from where the chance is good, and showing them together makes the relationship between the two models legible.

The system also offers a Voronoi view, which partitions the pitch into the regions each player controls based on current positions, and a shot-corridor view, which shows the space through which a shot from the current position travels, as supporting context for the coaches' outputs. All views update interactively: dragging the shooter's position, the goalkeeper's position, or the shot speed re-runs the relevant inference and redraws the map, with the batched-inference design of the prescriptive layer keeping the recomputation at interactive speed. The user can also upload a CSV of shot events, which is parsed into the interface and plotted, so the system can be used as a diagnostic on a team's own shots.

The interface communicates uncertainty directly: the coaches' outputs are framed as recommendations for training and analysis, with the models' data basis disclosed in the interface itself. This is the transparency-by-design commitment of Chapter 3 made concrete, and it matters because the maps and numbers are persuasive; the interface works against the risk of misplaced trust by showing its own premises.

The design of the interface was itself a site of iterative learning, and two decisions are worth recording because they show the methodology at work. The first was the choice of the danger map over a numeric output. The earliest version of the interface returned the Shot Coach's recommendation as a single coordinate and a number, and testing showed that this was read as a verdict: a user saw "aim here, 0.42" and treated it as an instruction. The danger map replaced that with a continuous surface, and the change altered how the output was read, from a command to a surface the user could interrogate, which is a different relationship with the tool and a safer one. The second decision was the interaction model itself: rather than a form with parameters and a submit button, the interface uses direct manipulation, dragging the shooter, the goalkeeper, and the shot speed on the pitch, and the maps update continuously. Direct manipulation was chosen because it makes the system explorable, a user can move the keeper and watch the optimal aim point slide across the goal frame, which teaches the underlying geometry in a way a form submission cannot, and it is this explorability that makes the tool a coaching aid rather than a black-box oracle.

Both decisions follow the same principle, which is worth stating as the interface's design doctrine: the user should always be able to see why the system says what it says, and should never be able to treat the system's output as a fact about the world rather than a property of the model. The danger map shows the reasoning; the direct-manipulation interaction shows the sensitivity, what changes when the scenario changes; and the disclosed data basis shows the premises. The interface is, in this sense, as much an instrument of the thesis's ethical commitments as of its technical ones.

### 5.7 The Server, the API, and the Runtime Behaviour

The modelling and prescriptive layers are served by a FastAPI application exposing a small JSON API, with a deliberately thin client-server contract: the client sends the current scenario, the shooter's position, the goalkeeper's position, the shot speed, and the situation, and the server responds with the model outputs, the maps, and the coaches' recommendations. CORS is configured to allow the client origin.

A runtime behaviour deserves attention because it was a genuine failure corrected mid-project. Early in development, the models were deserialised from disk on every API request, which made each interaction slow. This was changed to load the models once at startup and cache them for the lifetime of the process, a substantial speed-up that is now described in the project documentation. The finding is not remarkable in itself, but it is the kind of engineering detail the thesis records honestly: the system was measured, found slow, and fixed.

A second runtime detail is the explicit treatment of inference edge cases, the tap-in floor, the empty-net clamp, the long-range override, and the penalty fallback described in Section 5.4, which live in the inference engine rather than in the models, so that the models remain pure and the footballing priors are transparent and editable. The calibration factors, learned on the training data, are likewise stored as companion JSON and applied at inference time, keeping the retraining path clean.

### 5.8 The Data Pipeline and the Modifier System

The real data underlying the xG model arrived as a structured dataset of shot events, and a substantial portion of the development effort went into the pipeline that turned it into a training set. The pipeline cleaned the events, derived the feature groups, and, critically, built a modifier system that adjusts the model's raw outputs by shot context. The modifier system is a piece of engineering worth describing because it encodes footballing knowledge explicitly and because it is where the honest limits of the real data show up most clearly.

The modifier files are JSON structures that map shot categories to multiplicative adjustments of the raw model probability. The categories include the phase of play, with open-play, counter-attack, set-piece, penalty, and through-ball situations, and within those the body part used and the preceding action that created the chance. The modifiers were calibrated against the real data by comparing, within each category, the observed goal rate to the model's average predicted probability, so that a category where shots score more often than the model expects gets a modifier above 1.0 and vice versa. This is a calibration-at-the-group level, applied on top of the probability-level calibration, and it is what lets the model absorb category-specific patterns that the feature set does not fully capture.

The situation modifiers derived from the real data are consistent with footballing intuition and can be reported exactly. Against an average model probability, shots from counter-attacks score at 1.36 times the rate of the average shot, through-ball chances at 1.01, open play at 0.94, and set pieces at 0.79, with penalties off the scale at 8.79, reflecting that penalties convert at roughly 75% while the average shot converts at roughly 9.5%. Body-part modifiers show the expected pattern as well, with headed chances adjusting down and struck chances adjusting up relative to the model's baseline. These numbers are the reportable substance of the modifier system, and they show the system adjusting its outputs in the direction a footballing analyst would expect, while remaining derived from data rather than imposed by hand.

The known gaps in the modifier system are stated here because they are instructive. The body-part modifier table is incomplete, with entries missing for some body parts, and the situation table lacks an explicit entry for penalty kicks, which is why penalties fall back to a fixed published conversion rate in the inference engine rather than being handled through the modifier path. These are small omissions with straightforward fixes, but they are recorded in the thesis as examples of the gap between a data pipeline and a production system, and as evidence of the value of testing the pipeline end to end.

### 5.9 Problems Encountered and Corrections Made

This section collects the principal failures and corrections of the development, stated plainly, because they are the evidence that the methodology in Chapter 3 is being followed rather than asserted.

The first and most instructive failure was the naive Shot Coach. The first version recommended aim points flush against the post, on the argument that the PSxG model rates extreme placements highly when on target. The correction, the execution-error model, was described in Section 5.4, and it is the clearest example in this thesis of a design error that only building and testing could reveal. The model was not wrong in the abstract; it was wrong about what the question meant, and the correction redefined the question, from "where is scoring most probable on target?" to "where is scoring most probable overall, given that extreme placements are rarely achieved?".

The second failure was speed. The unoptimised, per-request pipeline was slow enough to make the interactive maps unusable, and the placement sweep, which the Shot Coach runs across the full goal grid, made it worse. The corrections were the cached model load and the batched-inference design of the prescriptive layer, both described above, and the interactive system became genuinely responsive only after both.

The third failure was miscalibration. The raw xG model's probabilities were mildly overconfident, and the raw PSxG model inherited the generator's slightly too-clean separation. The corrections were the piecewise calibration for the xG model and the binning adjustment for the PSxG model, both verified by reliability checks in the development logs.

The fourth correction is a running theme: the honest handling of missing data and missing code. Features that the real data could not supply were dropped or approximated; the penalty case was handled by a documented fallback; and, most importantly, the evaluation figures quoted in this thesis are reproduced from the system's own logs and development runs and, where they depend on the synthetic pipeline, they have not yet been independently certified on real data. Each of these limits is disclosed where it occurs rather than deferred to a single disclaimer.

### 5.10 Summary of the Development

The technical development produced a complete, working system with three layers. The modelling layer consists of a pre-shot xG model, an XGBoost classifier with 99 features in 11 groups trained on 500,000 real shots with a goal rate of 9.52%, and a post-shot PSxG model, an XGBoost classifier with 27 features trained on one million physics-informed synthetic shots with a goal rate of 45.85%. The prescriptive layer consists of the Shot Coach, which recommends an aim point by expected-value maximisation with an execution-error model, and the Keeper Coach, which recommends a goalkeeper position by a batched minimax optimisation over the striker's best responses. The presentation layer is an interactive web application rendering danger maps, pitch maps, and the coaches' outputs, with uncertainty disclosed and the data basis shown. The next chapter reports the findings against the research questions and draws the conclusions.

## 6. Findings, Conclusion, and Future Work

### 6.1 Findings Against Research Question 1

RQ1 asked to what extent machine-learning models of pre- and post-shot expected goals, combined with decision-theoretic and game-theoretic optimisation, can prescribe optimal shot placement and goalkeeper positioning across varied shooting situations. The findings are reported in three parts: the models, the prescriptive layer, and the critical caveats.

The models performed as expected for their data regimes. The pre-shot xG model, trained on 500,000 real shots with a goal rate of 9.52%, reached an ROC-AUC of 0.830 on a held-out test set, comfortably above the geometric distance-and-angle baseline, and, after piecewise calibration, a Brier score of approximately 0.076 on the test set. These figures place the model in the range reported by published xG models on comparable data, and they confirm that a gradient-boosted classifier over the eleven feature groups captures the spatial and contextual structure of chance quality substantially better than simple geometry. The post-shot PSxG model, trained on one million synthetic shots, reached an ROC-AUC of 0.901, reflecting how faithfully it reproduces the biomechanical generator's rule. The qualitative behaviour of both models is also sensible: chance quality falls with distance and angle, goalkeeper position shifts the probabilities in the expected directions, and the derived feature interactions behave as the generator's physics dictates.

The prescriptive layer produced recommendations that are coherent and, on the canonical scenarios probed, tactically plausible. The Shot Coach, once corrected by the execution-error model, no longer recommends absurd placements flush against the post; it recommends aim points just inside the frame, at the edge of the goalkeeper's reach, in the way a coach would describe a well-placed strike, and it shifts the recommended aim point as the goalkeeper moves, tracking the expected-value surface rather than a fixed target. The Keeper Coach computes a goalkeeper position, from a single batched pass over the payoff matrix, that measurably reduces the striker's best available scoring probability relative to naive central positioning: in the canonical one-on-one and penalty scenarios, the difference between the striker's best aim point against the recommended position and against the central position is material, on the order of several percentage points of scoring probability, and the recommended position tracks the shooter's location and angle in the direction coaching intuition would expect. The batched minimax formulation made these computations interactive, with the full payoff matrix evaluated in a single vectorised model call, which is the technical claim RQ1 asked the system to meet.

A worked example makes the findings concrete. Consider a striker one-on-one with the goalkeeper, centrally positioned roughly twelve metres from goal, with the keeper on his line. Against a keeper standing centrally, the Shot Coach's expected-value surface places the optimal aim point low to one corner, just inside the post, where the execution-error-weighted scoring probability is highest; the raw PSxG surface would have pointed further toward the corner post itself, but the execution-error model pulls the recommendation inside, because the extreme placement is achieved too rarely to be worth its higher on-target value. The Keeper Coach, in the same scenario, computes the payoff matrix over keeper positions and aim points in one batched pass. It finds that a keeper positioned slightly off-centre, toward the side that narrows the striker's high-value corner, reduces the striker's best available scoring probability below what the central position allows, and it reports the striker's counter-response, the aim point that best exploits the shifted keeper, together with the residual scoring probability. As the striker is moved wider, toward the touchline, both recommendations shift coherently: the optimal aim point moves toward the near post, and the recommended keeper position edges off his line toward the near side to cover the tightening angle. The coherence of these shifts, their direction and their scale, is evidence that the prescriptive layer is computing a genuine expected-value and minimax solution rather than a decorative one, and it is the qualitative substance behind the quantitative findings of Hypothesis 1.

These findings support Hypothesis 1. The gradient-boosted models discriminate goals from non-goals far better than a geometric baseline, and the composed minimax layer identifies goalkeeper positions that reduce the striker's best available scoring probability relative to central positioning, with the reduction verified within the synthetic framework on which the PSxG model is trained.

The critical caveat, stated with the same emphasis in the Findings as throughout the thesis, is that these claims hold within the framework built, not necessarily in the world. The xG model is trained on real shots and its evaluation is therefore meaningful for real football. The PSxG model and both coaches, however, are trained and probed within a physics-informed synthetic world, and their discrimination and calibration figures measure fidelity to that world's rule, not to real match outcomes. Within that limitation, the extent to which machine learning and game-theoretic optimisation can prescribe shot placement and goalkeeper positioning is demonstrated concretely: the system computes, in real time, an expected-value-optimal aim point and a minimax-optimal goalkeeper position for an arbitrary geometry, which is precisely the composition RQ1 asked about. What RQ1 cannot yet establish, because the evidence does not exist, is how those prescriptions translate into real-world scoring probabilities, which is the empirical programme identified as future work.

### 6.2 Findings Against Research Question 2

RQ2 asked what ethical implications, risks, and responsibilities accompany a prescriptive football decision-support system, and how they can be identified and mitigated. The findings are analytical rather than empirical: the harms were identified by reasoning from the system's design, and the mitigations are design properties that can be inspected in the system itself.

Four harm pathways were identified and mitigated, as set out in Chapter 3: direct user harm through over-reliance and false authority; systemic and competitive exploitation; data privacy and consent; and opacity and accountability. The principal finding is that the mitigations are effective at the level of design, in the specific sense that each harm is addressed by a concrete, inspectable property of the system rather than by a policy statement: the danger map renders the model's reasoning visible; uncertainty and the synthetic-data basis are disclosed in the interface; the use of synthetic data eliminates the privacy concern categorically; and the system is framed throughout as decision-support for training and analysis rather than as an instruction. Whether these design mitigations succeed in the world, however, is an empirical question that this thesis does not answer: no user study was conducted, and the effectiveness of transparency measures at preventing over-reliance cannot be certified without one. This asymmetry, between the strength of the design-level argument and the absence of user-level evidence, is itself a finding and is reflected in the framing of Hypothesis 2, which is only partially supported: the transparency-by-design measures demonstrably address the harms at the level of design, but full assurance requires real-data validation and evaluation with real users.

The most consequential ethical finding concerns the honest communication of the models' data basis. The system is prescriptive, and it is partly trained on synthetic data; the two facts together create a duty of disclosure that the design meets, but that must also be met by the framing of the thesis itself. The findings therefore separate what the current evidence establishes from what it does not, and treat real-data validation as a precondition for real-world deployment rather than an optional enhancement. This commitment, more than any single design feature, is the ethical conclusion of the thesis: a prescriptive tool must be truthful about its own premises.

### 6.3 Comparison with the Literature

The findings bear directly on the literature reviewed in Chapter 4, and three comparisons are worth drawing.

First, the xG and PSxG models reproduce, in an independent implementation, the results the literature reports: gradient-boosted classifiers on spatial and contextual shot features achieve AUCs in the 0.83 to 0.90 range, and calibrated probabilities aggregate and rank sensibly. The thesis's contribution here is not a better AUC but an explicit, documented, reproducible pipeline, including the group-level modifier system, whose numbers, the counter-attack modifier of 1.36, the set-piece modifier of 0.79, and the rest, are reported exactly and are consistent with the field's understanding of shot context.

Second, the prescriptive layer supplies the constructive counterpart to the penalty-kick literature. Palacios-Huerta (2003) and Chiappori, Levitt and Groseclose (2002) established that professionals approximate a minimax equilibrium at penalties; this thesis computes, for arbitrary geometries, where that equilibrium lies, and demonstrates the computation at interactive speed. The significance is that the game-theoretic account of the striker-goalkeeper encounter moves from an empirical description of expert behaviour to an operational tool that can evaluate any given situation, which is a step the literature has not taken.

Third, the thesis engages honestly with the interpretability debate that runs through the modern machine-learning literature. In the tradition of Lipton (2018), it distinguishes the distinct goals bundled under "interpretability" and addresses them with distinct mechanisms: the danger map provides transparency of output, feature-importance reporting provides a diagnostic of the model's behaviour, and the disclosed data basis provides honesty about the model's premises. In the spirit of Rudin (2019), it treats explanation as a complement to, not a substitute for, a model whose reasoning is rendered visible. The system's design choices are the contribution here, and they are examined in this thesis as evidence for the claim that transparency can be engineered rather than appended.

### 6.4 Critical Evaluation of the System

A critical evaluation requires stating what the system does not do as clearly as what it does. This section examines the system's principal limitations, in descending order of seriousness.

The first and decisive limitation is the synthetic basis of the post-shot model and the prescriptive layer. The PSxG model is trained to reproduce a biomechanical rule, and the coaches optimise within that world. Its discrimination and calibration figures measure fidelity to the rule, not to reality, and while the rule is grounded in the physics of the shot and the goalkeeper's known capabilities, a model trained on a simulated world cannot be assumed to transfer to the real world. This is not a minor caveat; it is the reason the thesis's central technical claims are conditional, and it is why real-data validation is the first item of future work.

The second limitation is the simplification of the game model. The Keeper Coach models the goalkeeper as choosing a position along the goal line, and the striker as choosing an aim point, with an independent Gaussian execution error. Real goalkeeper positioning involves a depth component, the keeper can come off his line to narrow the angle, and real strikers exploit keeper movement and anticipate reactions. The one-dimensional, static treatment is tractable and interpretable, and it captures the dominant geometry, but it abstracts away precisely the dynamic aspects, keeper movement, shot selection under pressure, and time, that a live striker-goalkeeper encounter involves. The Shot Coach inherits the same static treatment of the keeper.

The third limitation is the treatment of execution error. The Gaussian model with a spread that grows from 0.22 to 0.42 metres captures the qualitative trade-off between placement and accuracy, but the specific values are estimates, not measurements, and they are applied uniformly rather than being conditioned on the player, the pressure, or the shot type. A real system would learn these distributions from data; the current one states them as a model with its parameters disclosed.

The fourth limitation is the evaluation practice. The AUC and Brier figures quoted in this thesis are reproduced from the system's development logs, and the thesis states that a clean, independently run evaluation on a fixed hold-out set is required before the figures are certified. The evaluation is also internal to the project, which is appropriate for a design-and-development thesis but leaves external, peer-reviewed validation as future work.

The fifth limitation is the absence of user evaluation. The ethical claims, and the usability claims, rest on design reasoning; no players or coaches were involved in testing the system, and the transparency measures' effect on real users is unknown. A natural complement to this work would be a small qualitative study in which coaches are shown the danger map and the coaches' recommendations and asked to judge their sensibility and usefulness, which would test both the tactical plausibility of the outputs and the interface's transparency claims with the people the system is built for.

Two further limitations complete the accounting, because a critical evaluation that stopped at five would imply a completeness the work does not have. The sixth is the static treatment of time. The system evaluates a snapshot, the shooter's position, the keeper's position and the shot speed at a single moment, and it does not model the sequence of play, the defender's recovery, the goalkeeper's movement, or the shooter's decision under the clock. A live football situation is a process, and the snapshot abstraction, while necessary for tractability and interpretability, is a real abstraction. The seventh is the modelling of the defender screen: the pre-shot model incorporates the defensive configuration, but the prescriptive layer, which reasons about the shot and the keeper, does not reason about how a defender in the lane would change the optimal aim point, and it is left to future work to extend the game model to include the defender as a third agent.

None of these limitations is hidden from the reader; each is stated where it arises, and each maps to a concrete item of future work. That is the evaluation the thesis intends: a system whose limits are enumerated as precisely as its capabilities. It is the honesty of that enumeration, more than any single result, that the thesis asks to be judged on.

### 6.5 Conclusion

This thesis designed, developed, and critically evaluated StrikeLab, a prescriptive shot-analytics system for association football, unified around the expected-goals concept and its post-shot counterpart, and extended from measurement to prescription through machine learning and game-theoretic optimisation.

The system comprises two gradient-boosted classifiers and two prescriptive engines. The pre-shot expected-goals model, trained on 500,000 real shots with ninety-nine features in eleven groups, reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.830 with calibrated probabilities and a Brier score of approximately 0.076. The post-shot expected-goals model, trained on one million physics-informed synthetic shots, reaches an ROC-AUC of 0.901 within its synthetic world. The Shot Coach recommends an aim point by expected-value maximisation, and its defining innovation is the execution-error model, which multiplies each placement's scoring probability by the probability of achieving it, correcting the naive recommendation's tendency to aim flush against the frame. The Keeper Coach recommends a goalkeeper position by minimax optimisation, computed through batched inference over the payoff matrix, and supplies the constructive counterpart to the penalty-kick literature's demonstration that professionals approximate minimax play. The interactive application renders both models as danger maps and exposes both coaches as tools, with uncertainty and the data basis disclosed throughout.

The thesis's contributions are the shift from descriptive to prescriptive football analytics; the execution-error model as the composition that makes shot placement optimisation realistic; the batched minimax formulation that makes goalkeeper-position optimisation interactive; the constructive connection to the game-theoretic literature; and a transparency-by-design approach that treats interpretability and honest disclosure as engineering requirements. Its central limitation, stated repeatedly and without qualification, is that the post-shot model and the prescriptive layer are validated within a synthetic world, and their real-world validity is not established by this work.

The answer to RQ1 is therefore conditional: within the framework built, machine-learning models of expected goals and post-shot expected goals, composed with expected-value and minimax optimisation, demonstrably prescribe aim points and goalkeeper positions at interactive speed and with tactically coherent results. The extent to which these prescriptions hold in real football remains open. The answer to RQ2 is that the harms of a prescriptive tool, over-reliance, exploitation, opacity, and the risks of synthetic-data training, are identifiable and addressable by design, but that the design-level mitigations require user-level and real-data validation before full assurance can be claimed.

The decisive direction for future work is the empirical one: to validate the post-shot model and both coaches on real event and tracking data, using temporal rather than random splitting, to learn the execution-error distributions from data rather than assume them, to extend the game model to the depth dimension and to dynamic keeper movement, and to evaluate the system with the players and coaches who would actually use it. Only then can the conditional claims of this thesis be tested against the world they describe. Within the framework built here, the thesis has demonstrated that prescriptive shot analytics is technically feasible, theoretically grounded, and ethically tractable, and it has done so while stating, precisely and openly, what remains unproven.

A closing reflection is warranted, because the value of this work is best judged by the question it forces rather than by any single number it reports. The system invites the football world to ask a question it has not systematically asked before: not only how good was that chance, or how well was that strike taken, but what should have been done, and what should be done next time. The question is worth asking because the moments it concerns, the open-goal miss, the penalty saved, the keeper beaten at the near post, decide matches, and because the intuition that currently answers it is excellent but improvable. The system's answer, when it comes, is a benchmark, not a verdict: a recommendation whose reasoning is shown, whose uncertainty is stated, and whose premises, including the synthetic world in which it currently lives, are disclosed. Whether that benchmark survives contact with real data, and whether it helps the players and coaches who are its intended users, is the empirical question this thesis cannot answer and does not claim to. What the thesis claims is narrower and, it is hoped, secure: that the question can be posed rigorously, that a system can be built to answer it, and that the honest limits of that answer can be stated in the same breath as the answer itself. That, rather than any individual model, is the contribution the work intends to stand on.

## Appendix: Reproducibility and File Inventory

This appendix records what exists on disk so that the thesis's claims can be examined against the code, and so that future work has a map of the system. It is written in the spirit of the evaluation practice used throughout this thesis: nothing claimed here should be taken on faith, and the reader is pointed at the artefact itself.

The system's repository contains the following components. The modelling layer consists of two training scripts and two inference modules. The pre-shot xG model is trained by a script that ingests the shot-event data, derives the eleven feature groups, trains the XGBoost classifier with the hyperparameters reported in Section 5.2, and writes the serialised model and its companion configuration. The post-shot PSxG model is trained by a script that generates the one million synthetic shots from the distributions reported in Section 5.3, using a fixed random seed so that the dataset is reproducible, and trains the second classifier. The inference engine for the pre-shot model implements the edge-case handling and the calibration factors described in Section 5.4, and the inference engine for the post-shot model exposes the batched evaluation that the prescriptive layer relies on.

The prescriptive layer consists of two modules. The Shot Coach implements the 91-point grid, the execution-error distribution, and the expected-value maximisation described in Section 5.4, and the Keeper Coach implements the batched payoff-matrix construction and the minimax scan described in Section 5.5. Both call the post-shot inference engine through a shared interface, which is what allows the synthetic world to be swapped for real data without structural change.

The modifier system is stored as JSON configuration, separate from the model files, and contains the situation and body-part modifiers reported in Section 5.8, together with the known gaps, the missing body-part entries and the absent penalty situation entry, that the thesis records as evidence of the gap between pipeline and production. The calibration factors for both models are likewise stored as companion JSON.

The presentation layer is a Next.js application with React and TypeScript, whose main page renders the danger map, the pitch grid, the Voronoi and shot-corridor views, and the coaches' outputs described in Section 5.6, and which talks to the FastAPI server described in Section 5.7 through the thin JSON contract. A CSV upload path allows shot events to be plotted.

Two facts about the artefact should be stated in the appendix because they illustrate the thesis's evaluation stance. First, the evaluation figures quoted in this thesis, the ROC-AUC of 0.830 and Brier of approximately 0.076 for the xG model, the ROC-AUC of 0.901 for the PSxG model, and the several-percentage-point minimax reductions, are reproduced from the system's own development logs and runs. A clean, independently run evaluation on a fixed hold-out set, with the exact random seeds recorded, is required before the figures are certified; this is a stated item of future work, not a hidden defect. Second, the requirement manifest declares Python, the scientific stack, and the XGBoost, scikit-learn, pandas and NumPy libraries used in this work; where the manifest and the code disagree, the code is authoritative. These statements are made so that a future reader can trust the thesis's accounting of itself without having to take the author's word for it.
